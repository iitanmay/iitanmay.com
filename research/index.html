2. Mixing in gravity currents



A gravity current is a flow driven by a density (or buoyancy) difference between two fluids, which causes the denser fluid to spread horizontally along a surface. These flows are ubiquitous in nature and industry, from cold air spreading when you open a refrigerator door to large-scale phenomena like katabatic winds, undersea avalanches (turbidity currents), and volcanic pyroclastic flows.

The most fundamental setup to study them is the "lock-exchange" flow, where a barrier (or "lock") separating two fluids of different densities is suddenly removed. The denser fluid flows under the lighter one, and the lighter fluid flows over the denser one, creating two propagating "fronts."

My research focuses on two critical aspects of these flows: their propagation characteristics and the turbulent mixing they induce. I investigate these phenomena using a dual approach of high-fidelity numerical simulations (like LES) and advanced, high-resolution experiments.

Probing Turbulent Mixing in Lock-Exchange Flows

In a classic lock-exchange flow (uniform ambient), a key question is "how efficient is the mixing?" Understanding this is vital for predicting the dilution of pollutants or the transport of nutrients in an ocean.

To answer this, we used novel experimental techniques: Molecular Tagging Velocimetry (MTV) and Thermometry (MTT). This powerful combination allowed us to simultaneously measure both the velocity and temperature (density) fields at very small scales, capturing the intricate details of the turbulent flow.

Using this high-resolution data, we analyzed the flow through the background potential energy (BPE) framework. This method helps quantify the irreversible mixing—the portion of energy that actually goes into mixing the two fluids, rather than just stirring the fluid or being dissipated as heat. Our work successfully identified specific "vortical overturns," that are precursors to moments of high mixing efficiency.

Propagation Dynamics in a Stratified Environment

In nature, the ambient fluid is rarely uniform; it's often stratified (e.g., density changes with depth in the ocean or height in the atmosphere). My more recent work explores how this stratification changes the flow.

Using Large Eddy Simulations (LES), we studied buoyant currents propagating at the top surface of a linearly stratified medium. We found that the ambient stratification dramatically alters the flow dynamics.

The flow's behavior is controlled by the relative strength of the current's buoyancy and the ambient stratification.

  • Supercritical Flow: When stratification is weak, the current moves faster than the internal wave speed, generating a prominent solitary wave at its front.
  • Subcritical Flow: When stratification is strong, the current is slower and generates a field of vertically propagating internal waves, which "drain" energy from the flow.

This computational work was complemented by laboratory experiments using dye visualization to track the current's front, allowing us to validate the propagation speed scaling laws we developed from the simulations.

By combining these experimental and computational approaches, my research aims to build a more complete physical model of how gravity currents propagate, mix, and transport mass in both simple and complex environmental settings.
